5.7× at 64K: writing FlashAttention-2 in Triton

Notes from Where the Time and Memory Go — systems measurements on single and multi-GPU A6000 boxes, driven by a profiling pass over 128M–3.4B-parameter models.

The profiling set the agenda before any kernel was written. Two facts stood out: attention's softmax is 51× cheaper than matmul in FLOPs yet costs comparable time, and the T×T score matrix is the single largest memory consumer in the step. So attention got a from-scratch FlashAttention-2: a naive PyTorch reference, a Triton forward with online softmax, and a Triton two-pass backward that recomputes the scores and needs no atomics.

What it bought:

  • Sweeping 128 → 65,536 tokens (head dim 64, batch 1, causal, one A6000): at 64K in bf16, flash is 5.7× faster end-to-end (forward + backward). In fp32 at the same length, naive attention runs out of memory while flash completes.
  • An Nsight Systems trace at 2,048 tokens shows where it comes from. Naive attention: 314 kernels, 4.82 ms, with elementwise operations at 50% of GPU time, softmax at 19%, and the three matmuls at 28%. Flash: 105 kernels, 2.65 ms, 95% of it in three purpose-written kernels. The speedup is fusion, avoided HBM round-trips for the score matrix, and fewer launches.
  • Two honest caveats: time still scales O(S²) — flash saves memory, not computation — and the backward pass recomputes the scores twice, costing ~3.5× the forward.

From writing the kernels. tl.dot requires both operands in the same dtype, so the bf16 path casts the probability tile explicitly; at head dimension 128 the backward kernel exceeds the shared-memory budget and needs smaller tiles with num_stages=1; and a two-pass backward that recomputes the scores twice beats a one-pass formulation with atomics.

The rest of the stack, same report. BF16 autocast: 1.8–3.2× on forward passes. Gradient checkpointing: 0.37× peak memory at ~2× compute — with the trap that checkpointing everything at once is worse than not checkpointing (1.14×), because the backward pass materializes every block's residuals simultaneously; block size 1 is optimal here. DDP with overlap: communication share 69% → 59% on 4 GPUs — and flattening the collectives changed nothing (790 vs 788 ms/step), because the cost is the bytes moved, not the number of calls. ZeRO-1: −23.7% step memory for +14.2% step time, not the naive 50% — only the optimizer state is sharded. FSDP: −43% steady-state memory, within 14% of PyTorch's own FSDP.

The takeaway. Matmul holds over 60% of a forward pass's FLOPs but under 30% of a step's kernel time — follow time and byte traffic, not operation counts. And every optimization moves the bottleneck rather than removing it: activations cap context length, optimizer state caps batch size per replica, communication caps device count.

Caveats: the headline attention sweep is single-GPU, head dimension 64, batch 1; the distributed numbers are small multi-GPU runs, not a cluster. Full report and code: llm-from-scratch.
