A verifier that's wrong 10% of the time: RL shrugs, best-of-N doesn't

Notes on a controlled experiment from Prompt Dominance and Asymmetric Verifier Costs — a from-scratch GRPO study at 1B scale, now on arXiv (2610.04928).

When you train a language model against a reward verifier — a script that checks whether the final answer is right — the same verifier usually does two jobs. It provides the training reward, and at test time it picks which of N sampled answers to submit. These two jobs are usually studied separately. I wanted them side by side, under exactly the same amount of verifier damage.

The setup. OLMo-2-0425-1B, GSM8K, GRPO implemented from scratch. The verifier is a symbolic-equality grader: it extracts the answer from \boxed{} and compares it to the reference. To damage it, I flipped each verdict with probability ε — identically in training and at selection time. I also ran a verifier that only checks the answer's format.

What happened:

  • At a 10% flip rate, RL training retains 91% and 106% of its attainable gain (two seeds). Test-time selection over 32 samples retains 57%.
  • At a 30% flip rate, RL retains about 70% of the gain where selection retains 14% (matched-step comparison on the internal validation split).
  • With a format-only verifier, RL keeps 16–30% of its gain and selection keeps essentially nothing.

Why the asymmetry. A flip is an affine perturbation of the expected reward, and the group-normalized advantage that GRPO uses — together with Adam's rescaling — removes it almost exactly; what survives is a second-order variance effect. Selection has no such protection: it takes an argmax over the top of the ranking, where the candidates are closest together, so a single flipped verdict can pick the wrong answer outright.

The takeaway. If your verifier is noisy, spend that noise on training, not on inference-time selection. Best-of-N is a free robustness win only when the verifier is exact; against verifier noise it's the fragile one.

One more thing the same study found. The prompt is the first-order decision, before any of this matters. With a zero-shot prompt, the base model scored 0.08% — its outputs had the shape of an answer with no answer inside, so almost no group carried a gradient. A 3-shot prompt scored 18.3% and training worked. If an RL run is stuck, check the prompt before touching the optimizer.

Everything here is measured at 1B scale; the mechanism should generalize, the numbers are what I measured. Full report, code, and the raw run logs behind every figure: llm-from-scratch.
