What a crawl filter actually removes: 2.14M documents in, 340M tokens out

Notes from From Crawl to Corpus — a Common Crawl filtering pipeline built from scratch and audited document by document.

The pipeline is the modern stack, implemented from scratch: HTML-to-text extraction with encoding detection → fastText language identification → regular-expression PII masking → Dolma's Jigsaw NSFW and hate-speech classifiers → the Gopher quality heuristics → a fastText quality classifier → exact line deduplication → MinHash+LSH document deduplication. Input: 100 WET shards, 2,135,796 documents, 6.2 GB.

The funnel, and what each stage really does:

  • Language identification removes 70.3% — only 30% of the raw shards are English. The single largest filter by far.
  • The quality classifier removes 16.8%; the Gopher heuristics 3.5%; the two safety classifiers together 0.05%.
  • Kept: 9.3% — 198,764 documents. Line deduplication then cuts 57.5% of the surviving lines (navigation bars and footers are near-universal), and MinHash+LSH, run last, removes 52 more documents (0.03%). Almost all the redundancy is already gone by then; MinHash's value is on unfiltered corpora.
  • The audit is where the aggregate numbers break down. Of 25 raw WET records read before filtering, about half were machine-generated SEO, adult, or gambling pages, and roughly one in five resembled a genuine article. Then, reading kept and discarded samples with scores recomputed:
  • The safety classifiers are English-trained and nearly blind to everything else: sampled Chinese adult pages scored 0.920 and 0.915 against the 0.9 cutoff — barely over — and one page at 0.687 slipped under it, removed only because the second classifier also fired.
  • The Gopher rules are English-specific and punish the wrong things: whole CJK documents fail the mean-word-length test (a whitespace artifact, not a content signal), and WET's sidebar listings make good blog posts fail the alphabetic-word rule — all four sampled rejections failed on that rule with ratios of 54–72% while their prose was fine.

Does filtering help the model? A paired 4,000-step ablation — identical initial weights, identical validation batches — trains one model on the filtered corpus and one on a language-filter-only control. The filtered corpus reaches 0.27 nats lower validation loss at the best checkpoint (5.64 vs 5.91; perplexity 282 vs 367), with the gap widening over training (0.20 → 0.358 nats) and well outside the run's own scatter (below 0.01 nats). Two facts belong attached to that number: the quality classifier's positives came from the benchmark's own validation bin, so part of the gain is agreement with that distribution; and the control saw three times more tokens, which works against the filtered arm — making 0.27 a lower bound on the effect of filtering itself. A telltale of what the control is doing with that extra data: its training loss drops below the filtered arm's from step 300 onward while its validation loss stays worse — the signature of memorizing redundant text.

The takeaway. "Removes X%" is a necessary number, not a sufficient one — read what the filters drop. And evaluate a corpus by training on it.

Scale: 100 shards, 6.2 GB, 2.14M documents; extrapolated to 2,500 shards, this pipeline yields ≈8.5B tokens in about 10 hours, mostly unattended. Full report and the measurement tables behind the ablation: llm-from-scratch.
