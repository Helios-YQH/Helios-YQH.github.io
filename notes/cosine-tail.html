The cosine tail hides divergence: a run that explodes and recovers

Notes from A Transformer Language Model From Scratch — a 22.7M-parameter model and one deliberately aggressive learning-rate sweep.

The recipe is standard: linear warmup, then cosine decay to 10⁻⁵. For this setup the optimal learning rate is 3×10⁻³ at batch size 64 (2×10⁻³ at 256). I extended the sweep upward to 3.0 — a thousand times the optimum — running each with the same 1,000-step schedule and recording the final validation loss.

What happened:

  • The final losses rise monotonically across the extended sweep — 3.33, 3.96, 4.07, 4.05, 4.78, 5.20, 6.21 from 2×10⁻² to 3.0 — and not one of the runs ends in NaN. Read only that column and you would conclude this model tolerates a thousandfold learning rate.
  • The trajectories say otherwise. At lr 1.0 the loss peaks around 400 at step 300; at 3.0, around 6,000. Both then descend by orders of magnitude as the cosine tail anneals the learning rate back down. The divergence is real; the final number hides it, because the schedule guarantees every run ends at a tiny learning rate.
  • The control settles it: the 0.5 run, rerun with the schedule flattened (constant rate after warmup), never recovers — after the same step-300 explosion it swings between single digits and 470 and ends at 88, two orders of magnitude above anything the annealing schedules produce.
  • So the divergence threshold here is around 0.5 (≈170× the optimum), and it has to be read off the trajectory — a final-loss table gets this exactly backwards.
  • One more thing the same report shows: the threshold is a property of the whole recipe, not the learning rate. Removing RMSNorm and changing nothing else breaks training at the optimal learning rate — loss ~9.2 by step 80, NaN by step 240.

The takeaway. Don't compare runs by final loss across different schedules — a cosine tail is a very effective broom. Plot validation curves on a log scale, and when an ablation seems to recover, look at the middle of the run before believing it.

For calibration, the main run: 22.7M parameters, batch 256, lr 2×10⁻³, 5,000 steps — 327.7M tokens in 1.5 hours on one A6000 — reaching 1.57 validation loss. That is short of the track's 1.45 reference target; the gap is budget, not a bug (the schedule ends while the loss is still descending), and the comparisons above are the point of the setup rather than the absolute number.

Caveats: validation loss is itself a random quantity at the 10⁻³ level (mean over 10 batches; no error bars), and run-to-run noise without a fixed seed is ~0.06 nats. Full report and code: llm-from-scratch.
